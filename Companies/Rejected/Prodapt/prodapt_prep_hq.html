01 Reality check

Read this before touching anything else. It is the reason the plan looks the way it does.

The prep that was in progress does not map to this paper. Trees, BFS, level-order traversal and binary search — the last two days of work — appear nowhere in Prodapt's sample paper. Not one question. That work is not wasted for Viasat in October, but it earns approximately zero marks here.
The one algorithmic gap that does matter is DP. Problem 2 of the coding section is a string-partition dynamic program. Placement Training/DSA/ currently has zero DP files. That is the single highest-value algorithm to build before the 5th — and it is worth exactly one problem, so it does not get the whole plan either.
The overlap with TCS is the real argument. TCS (16 Aug) is your stated primary goal and is also quant / reasoning / verbal heavy. Every hour spent on DI, data sufficiency, critical reasoning and percentages counts twice. Prodapt prep is TCS prep. That is not a rationalisation — it is why this ordering wins.

04 Data Interpretation

Highest-priority section and currently at zero coverage. The sample's DI is arithmetic-heavy rather than clever — the marks are lost to setup errors and clock, not to difficulty.

Pie charts in degrees in paper

The sample gives sector values in degrees, total marks 900. Every question needs marks = degrees × 900 / 360 first — i.e. × 2.5.

  • Convert once, write all five values in the margin, then answer.
  • Ratio questions ("A+B less than C+D by what %") need no conversion at all — degrees are proportional.
  • Prove it: a sector of 55° with total 900 — what are the marks?

Multi-currency costing in paper

Parts priced in local currencies, a USD exchange table, plus a flat INR 20,000 per car.

  • Normalise everything to one currency before comparing anything.
  • The flat add-on is a red herring for "which country is cheapest" — it's constant.
  • Prove it: why does the INR 20,000 not affect the cheapest-country answer?

Ratio caselets in paper

1650 students, M:F = 86:79, five departments, facts given as chained dependencies.

  • Draw the 5×3 grid (dept × M / F / total) and fill only what's stated.
  • Work the most-constrained cell first, never left-to-right.
  • Prove it: 86:79 of 1650 — how many males? (parts sum to 165, so 1 part = 10)

Machine scheduling in paper

3 machines × 3 products, minutes per unit, 720 min/machine/day.

  • "Maximum units" = assign each machine the product it makes fastest.
  • Worked answer in the paper is 212 = 72 + 80 + 60.
  • Prove it: why does maximising count mean minimising per-unit time?

Venn / set logic in paper

150 students, 90 football, 60 basketball, 30 both.

  • Only-A = A − both. Neither = Total − (A + B − both).
  • The trap: "like Football" includes the 30, "only Football" doesn't.
  • Prove it: how many like neither? (150 − 120 = 30)

Clock discipline tactic

DI sets share a stem across 3–5 questions — the setup cost is paid once.

  • Never abandon a set after doing the setup work; that's the sunk cost you want.
  • Do skip a set whose stem looks heavy before you've invested in it.

05 Logical Reasoning & Critical Thinking

The widest section, and the one with two formats you have not met: data sufficiency and GMAT-style critical reasoning. Both are learnable in an evening because they're rule-driven.

Data sufficiency — memorise the grid

You are not asked to answer the question. You are asked whether it can be answered. Solving it fully is the classic time sink.

OptionMeans
AI alone sufficient, II alone not
BII alone sufficient, I alone not
CEither alone is sufficient
DBoth together still not sufficient
EBoth together needed (neither alone)
Method: test statement I alone → eliminate. Test II alone → eliminate. Only then consider them together. Never carry knowledge from I into your test of II — that single leak causes most wrong answers in this format.

Critical reasoning — four question types, four different jobs

Assumption

An unstated fact the argument needs to be true. Negate the option — if the argument collapses, that's the assumption.

Weaken

Attack the link between evidence and conclusion, not the evidence itself. The right answer often supplies an alternative cause.

Must be true

Provable only from the stated text. Anything requiring outside knowledge or a plausible extra step is wrong, however reasonable.

Explain the discrepancy

Two facts that seem to conflict. The answer is a third fact making both true at once — it does not pick a side.

Coding-decoding — the sample's actual rule

Given 'given time simple plan' → '@E4 &N4 %N5 #E6', the rule decomposes into three independent parts. Derive each separately rather than trying to see the whole code at once:

# given(5) time(4) simple(6) plan(4)  ->  @E4  &N4  %N5  #E6
symbol  ->  position of the word in the sentence  (@ = 1st, & = 2nd, % = 3rd, # = 4th)
letter  ->  LAST letter of the word               (giveN, timE, simplE, plaN)
digit   ->  LENGTH of the word

# check against row 3: 'sick point good turn' -> '#K4 %D4 @N4 &T5'
# turn is 4th word... but carries & (2nd). So the CODES ARE SHUFFLED,
# not aligned to the word order. Match each code to its word by
# last-letter + length, then read the symbol off. That is the real trap.

06 Quantitative Aptitude

Eight questions in the sample, all standard placement quant. Three carry a specific trap worth pre-loading.

CI over 2 years 4 months in paper

Fractional periods break naive compounding. Compound for the whole years, then apply simple interest on the resulting amount for the leftover fraction.

A = P(1+r)² × (1 + r×(4/12))

Savings % change in paper

Spends 60% → saves 40%. Income +21%, expenditure +5%. Take income = 100 and just compute: new savings = 121 − 63 = 58, from 40. That's +45%.

Assume 100. Never do this one symbolically.

Profit as % of SP in paper

Profit = 320% of cost → CP 100, profit 320, SP 420. Cost rises 25% → CP 125, SP fixed at 420, profit 295. As % of SP: 295/420 ≈ 70%.

Trap: the question switches the base from CP to SP mid-way.

Weighted average interest in paper

Total interest from known parts, subtract from overall, divide by the remaining principal. Find the leftover principal first — 20000 − 4000 − 8000 − 2800.

Averages logic in paper

"Average of 20 numbers is zero — at most how many exceed zero?" Answer 19: one sufficiently negative number balances the rest. Tests reasoning, not computation.

Syllogism in paper

No A are B; no B are C. Nothing follows about A–C. "Some grapefruits are not tangerine" does follow from "no tangerine is grapefruit". Draw the circles — don't reason verbally.

07 Verbal Reasoning — literally GRE

The sample paper's verbal pages are headed "Sample GRE® General Test Questions". This is a higher bar than typical placement verbal, and the scoring rule makes it harsher still.

No partial credit. Text Completion with three blanks needs all three correct for any marks. Sentence Equivalence needs both choices. Getting two of three earns exactly the same as getting zero of three.

Text Completion

1–3 blanks, three choices each. Find the sentence's directional pivot first — although, nonetheless, because, yet — then predict your own word before reading options.

Sample answer set: overshadowed / obscure / superficiality of our theories.

Sentence Equivalence

Six options, pick two producing sentences with the same meaning. Two options are near-synonyms of each other — find that pair. A word that fits but has no partner is a trap.

RC — select one or more

Each of three statements is judged independently; all correct ones must be selected. Verify each against the passage separately.

Select-in-Passage

Click the sentence doing a specific job. Match the function asked for ("distinguishes two ways") rather than topic keywords.

Vocabulary is the binding constraint here. Placement material/Norman Lewis - Word Power Made Easy is already in the repo — two chapters a day between now and the 4th is the realistic move.

08 Technical MCQ

Deliberately broad rather than deep — Java, SQL, Angular, unit-test mocking and digital logic all appear. Each is one or two questions, so breadth beats depth.

Java · from the paper 
public class A { int add(int i, int j){ return i+j; } }
public class B extends A{
  public static void main(String argv[]){
    short s = 9;
    System.out.println(add(s,6));
  }
}
A) error on line 2   B) non-static method from static context   C) type mismatch   D) 15
B — compilation fails: add is an instance method called from static main with no object. The short → int widening is legal and is the distractor. Inheritance doesn't help — main is static, so there's no this.
SQL · from the paper Which statement about primary keys is not correct?
A) Uniquely identifies each record   B) Must contain unique values   C) Can contain NULL, but undesirable for performance   D) A table can have only one
C — a primary key can never contain NULL. It's a correctness constraint, not a performance preference. The option is phrased to sound like a nuanced tradeoff. That phrasing is the trap.
Mockito · from the paper Which snippet correctly mocks CalculatorManager inside Calculator?
A) Calculator calculator = new Calculator(); with @Mock on the manager
B) @InjectMocks Calculator calculator with @Mock on the manager
B — @InjectMocks is what pushes the mock into the object under test. In A the mock is created but never injected, so Calculator still builds its own real CalculatorManager internally and the stubbing has no effect.
Digital logic · from the paper 2-input XOR, A = 1 and B = 1. Output?
A) 0   B) 1   C) −1   D) None
A) 0 — XOR is 1 only when inputs differ. Also memorise NAND: output 0 only when both inputs are 1; 1 in all three other rows.

09 Coding — three problems, one of them hard

Order of attack is the whole strategy here. Two problems are near-free; one is a genuine dynamic program. Bank the free ones before touching the third.

1 · winner(erica, bob) trivial

Each character is E / M / H with points by difficulty. Sum both, compare, return "Erica", "Bob" or "Tie". n < 10.

# the only real decision is the points map — the paper's
# scoring table is an image, so READ IT ON THE DAY.
# E/M/H = 1/3/5 is the common convention; do not assume it.
pts = {"E":1, "M":3, "H":5}
e = sum(pts[c] for c in erica)
b = sum(pts[c] for c in bob)
return "Erica" if e>b else "Bob" if b>e else "Tie"

3 · removeVowels(N, S) trivial + 1 trap

Remove vowels occurring at the end of S. Both cases count. N ≤ 1e5.

The trap is the whole problem. It is not "remove all vowels". It is "strip the trailing run of vowels". "education" → "education" (ends in n, nothing to strip), not "dctn". 
i = len(S)
while i > 0 and S[i-1] in "aeiouAEIOU":
    i -= 1
return S[:i]

# "beautiful" -> "beautiful"   (ends in l)
# "India"     -> "Ind"         (a, i, ... wait: 'a' then 'i' -> strips "ia")
# guaranteed non-empty output, so no empty-string guard needed

2 · calculateMinCost(N, K, S) hard — partition DP

Split S into exactly K contiguous parts. A part's cost is the number of ways to choose two equal substrings inside it (overlaps allowed). Minimise the total. N ≤ 200, K ≤ 10.

Two independent pieces — build and test them separately:

# STEP 1 — cost[i][j] for every substring S[i..j], O(N^2) of them.
# For a part, group its substrings by content; a group of size m
# contributes C(m,2) = m*(m-1)/2 pairs. Sum over all groups.
# N=200 means ~20k substrings per part — brute force is too slow if
# done naively per part; precompute with rolling hashes or by
# extending j one char at a time and maintaining counts.

# STEP 2 — the partition DP itself, the standard shape:
#   dp[k][i] = min cost to split the first i chars into k parts
#   dp[k][i] = min over j < i of  dp[k-1][j] + cost[j][i-1]
#   dp[0][0] = 0, everything else starts at infinity
#   answer = dp[K][N]
# Complexity O(N^2 * K) = 200*200*10 = 400k. Comfortable.
Partial credit strategy. If step 1 defeats you, still submit the DP with a brute-force cost function. It will pass the small cases and time out on the big ones — which scores more than an empty function. Never leave this blank.

Verify the given example before trusting your cost function: the part "aaaa" must come out as 10 (6 single-char pairs + 3 "aa" pairs + 1 "aaa" pair).

Confirm the language options on the day. The MCQs use Java, SQL and Angular, so Java is certainly supported. Python is not evidenced either way in the sample paper — check before you commit, since your solutions are all Python.

10 Prompt Engineering

Added 4 Aug from an official Prodapt notice: "Students are advised to have a basic understanding of Prompt Engineering, including how to write effective prompts for Generative AI tools. This knowledge may be useful during the selection process."

Read the wording carefully — it says "basic understanding" and "may be useful". That is interview-conversation and MCQ territory, not a deep technical exam. Prodapt is a telecom engineering services firm; the plausible framings are a technical-interview question ("how would you prompt an LLM to do X?"), an MCQ on terminology, or a short written task. Do not spend the night on this — 45 minutes gets you everything the phrasing implies.

The anatomy of an effective prompt

Five components. Naming them is most of what "basic understanding" means, and it's what makes an interview answer sound structured rather than vague.

ComponentWhat it doesWeak vs strong
TaskThe actual instruction, stated as a verb"about logs" → "extract every ERROR line and group by service"
ContextBackground the model can't inferadd the log format, the system, what the output feeds into
ConstraintsBoundaries and rules"only the last 24 h", "do not invent service names"
Output formatExact shape of the answer"JSON array with keys service, count, first_seen"
ExamplesOne or two input→output pairsturns a zero-shot prompt into few-shot

Named techniques — know the vocabulary

Zero-shot

Instruction only, no examples. Fine for common, well-specified tasks.

Few-shot

Two or three input→output examples. The fastest fix when output format or style is wrong — showing beats describing.

Chain-of-thought

"Reason step by step before answering." Helps on multi-step logic and arithmetic; largely wasted on retrieval or formatting.

Role / persona

"You are a network engineer reviewing a BGP config." Sets vocabulary and depth. Useful, and routinely overrated — it never substitutes for a clear task.

Decomposition

Split one large ask into chained smaller prompts, each verifiable. The single most practical technique in real work.

Iterative refinement

Treat the first output as a draft; state precisely what was wrong and re-prompt. Say this out loud in an interview — it shows you've actually used these tools.

Before and after

# WEAK — vague task, no context, no format, no constraints
"Write code to process the customer data."

# STRONG — all five components present
"You are reviewing a Python ETL script for a telecom billing pipeline.   <- role
Read the CSV below, which has columns msisdn, plan_id, usage_mb, and     <- context
may contain duplicate msisdn rows.
Write a function that de-duplicates by msisdn, keeping the row with the  <- task
highest usage_mb.
Use only the standard library, and do not modify the input list.        <- constraints
Return the function only, with type hints and no explanation."          <- output format

Failure modes worth naming

  • Ambiguity — "summarise this" without saying for whom, how long, or what to keep.
  • Buried instruction — the real ask sits in the middle of a wall of context.
  • No verification path — nothing in the output tells you whether it's right.
  • Hallucination risk left open — no instruction to say "not in the source" when the answer isn't there.
  • Over-stuffing — five unrelated tasks in one prompt. Decompose instead.
If asked about limitations, don't oversell. A good answer names the real ones: models hallucinate confidently, they can't verify their own output, prompt quality doesn't fix a model that lacks the knowledge, and anything touching customer data raises privacy questions — which for a telecom services company is the answer they're most likely listening for.

11 Drill deck

Questions in the paper's own style. Attempt before revealing — a revealed answer you didn't try teaches nothing.

DI · pie chart in degrees A pie chart shows marks by subject with sector values in degrees; total marks are 900. Mathematics occupies 100°. What are the Mathematics marks, and what would they be if the total were 3000?
250 marks; 833⅓ at a total of 3000. degrees × total / 360. The second part needs no re-derivation — scale by 3000/900.
Quant · savings A man spends 60% of his income. Income rises 21%, expenditure rises 5%. Percentage increase in savings?
A) 60%   B) 18%   C) 40%   D) 45%
D) 45% Income 100 → 121. Expenditure 60 → 63. Savings 40 → 58. 18/40 = 45%. Assume 100; never do this symbolically.
Reasoning · data sufficiency How many children are between Priya and Rashmi in a row?
I. Priya is fifteenth from the left.   II. Rashmi is exactly in the middle with ten children to her right.
E — both statements are needed. II fixes the row length (21) and Rashmi's position (11th). I fixes Priya's. Neither alone locates both people. The paper confirms E.
Reasoning · averages If zero is the average of 20 numbers, at most how many can be greater than zero?
19 A single sufficiently negative number can offset nineteen positives. Not all 20 — they couldn't sum to zero.
Reasoning · series 2, 1, (1/2), (1/4), …   and   SCD, TEF, UGH, ____, WKL
1/8, and VIJ. First: halving each time. Second: each letter advances — S→T→U→V, C→E→G→I, D→F→H→J.
CR · explain the discrepancy Median house value in Butlerville has fallen significantly, yet homes sold are at a seven-year low. Which best explains this?
The local economy is struggling and unemployment has hit historic rates. A third fact making both observations true at once. "Value isn't the only determinant" restates the puzzle rather than resolving it — a very common wrong-answer shape.
Coding · trap check removeVowels on "HELLO", "aeiou" is impossible (output guaranteed non-empty), and on "Programme"?
"HELL" and "Programm". Only the trailing run goes. Interior vowels survive. If you returned "PRGRMM" you solved a different problem.
Venn 150 students: 90 like football, 60 basketball, 30 both. How many like only basketball, and how many like neither?
30 only basketball; 30 neither. 60 − 30 = 30. Union = 90 + 60 − 30 = 120, so 150 − 120 = 30.

12 Mock protocol — 4 Aug

The mock's only job is calibration. Run it wrong and it tells you nothing.

Conditions

  • Single unbroken sitting, phone in another room
  • No lookups, no restarts, no "I'll redo that one"
  • Same section order as the paper: DI → LR → Coding/MCQ → Quant → Verbal
  • Timing is unknown, so impose your own: 2 hours total, and note where you ran out

Marking

  • Score each section separately — the aggregate hides everything useful
  • For every miss write the cause: misread / didn't know / ran out of time / arithmetic
  • Those four causes need four different fixes; the topic label needs none
  • Day 4 drills only the misses. Nothing new enters after the mock.
The thing this actually tests. Every problem solved this week has been solved with a tutor in the loop. The mock is the first unassisted measurement — and unassisted performance is the only number the drive will read.
