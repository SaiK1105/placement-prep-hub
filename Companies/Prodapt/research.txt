  - Caselet: 1650 students, male:female 86:79, five departments, chained

  - Venn probability over prime vs even number sets.

--- 2. LOGICAL REASONING & CRITICAL THINKING -------------------------
  - Constraint scheduling (7 flower types across 3 seasons, 5 rules).
  - Circular seating, 8 people facing centre, partial-info deductions.
  - Statement + Conclusions ("which conclusion follows").
  - GMAT-style CRITICAL REASONING, and this is the heaviest sub-block:
      * assumption questions   (20th-century art / "beautiful"; Dvorak)
      * must-be-true           (impulse purchase study)
      * weaken the conclusion  (Dvorak keyboard adoption)
      * explain the discrepancy (Butlerville house values vs sales)
  - Number series: 2, 1, 1/2, 1/4, ...
  - Letter series: SCD, TEF, UGH, ___, WKL
  - Odd-one-out (dodge / flee / duck / avoid)
  - Courses of action (car booking queues)
  - Analogy pairs (8:4)
  - CODING-DECODING, non-trivial construction:
      'given time simple plan' -> '@E4 &N4 %N5 #E6'
      Decoded rule: symbol encodes word position in the sentence,
      letter = last letter of the word, digit = word length.
  - DATA SUFFICIENCY, 5-option format (I alone / II alone / either /
    neither / both needed). Four worked examples with answers given.

--- 3. PROBLEM SOLVING — CODING & MCQ --------------------------------
  MCQ topics found (this is a wide, non-DSA spread):
   - Java OOP output prediction: calling a non-static method from a
     static context; short -> int widening. (Answer hinges on the
     static-context error, not the arithmetic.)
   - SQL: primary key properties (which statement is FALSE — the NULL
     one); and a join-vs-subquery correctness question comparing
     customer city to order city.
   - Angular / TypeScript: *ngIf with "as value; else elseBlock",
     chained pipes (uppercase | lowercase).
   - Testing: Mockito / JUnit — @RunWith, @Mock vs @InjectMocks.
     Correct answer is the @InjectMocks variant.
   - Digital logic: 2-input NAND truth table; 2-input XOR with A=1,B=1.

  CODING — exactly three problems in the sample:
   (1) "winner" — strings erica[], bob[], each char in {E,M,H}, score by
       difficulty, return "Erica" / "Bob" / "Tie". n < 10.
       DIFFICULTY: trivial. Pure loop + dict.
   (2) "calculateMinCost" — partition string S into exactly K
       contiguous parts; cost of a part = number of ways to choose two
       equal substrings within it (overlaps allowed); minimise total.
       N <= 200, K <= min(N,10).
       DIFFICULTY: HARD. Partition DP, O(N^2 K) with a precomputed
       part-cost table. This is the only hard item in the whole paper.
   (3) "removeVowels" — remove vowels occurring AT THE END of S.
       N <= 1e5, both cases count as vowels.
       DIFFICULTY: trivial, but note it is a TRAILING RUN, not all
       vowels. Misreading this is the entire risk.

  Interpretation: two free problems and one hard one. Sequencing
  matters more than algorithmic depth — bank (1) and (3) first.

--- 5. VERBAL REASONING ----------------------------------------------
[A] The verbal section of the sample paper is taken VERBATIM from
    official GRE General Test sample questions — the pages are even
    headed "Sample GRE(R) General Test Questions".

  Formats present:
   - Reading Comprehension, select ONE answer
   - Reading Comprehension, select ONE OR MORE answers, no partial
     credit
   - Select-in-Passage (click the sentence that does X)
   - Text Completion with 1-3 blanks, three choices per blank,
     NO partial credit
   - Sentence Equivalence — pick TWO choices producing equivalent
     sentences, no partial credit

  Implication: this is a materially higher verbal bar than the usual
  TCS/Infosys-style verbal, and the no-partial-credit rule means
  vocabulary gaps are expensive. Passages seen: carbon sequestration
  economics; composer Philip Glass.
