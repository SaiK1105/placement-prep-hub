Bias-Variance & Regularization

This cluster is the most-escalated topic in ML interviews. Everyone can define overfitting. The separator is whether you can decompose the error and explain why each fix works.
DeriveDecompose the expected prediction error into bias, variance, and noise. Why is there a tradeoff?
For squared loss, expected test error at a point decomposes as: 
E[(y - f̂(x))²]  =  Bias[f̂(x)]²  +  Var[f̂(x)]  +  σ²
Bias = error from wrong assumptions — how far your average prediction (over many training sets) is from truth. Variance = how much your prediction changes if you retrain on a different sample. σ² = irreducible noise, the floor you can never beat. Why they trade off: both are controlled by model flexibility. A more flexible model fits the training data more closely — that reduces systematic error (bias) but makes the fit more sensitive to which particular points you sampled (variance). You're moving along one axis, so pushing one down pushes the other up. The line that lands: "Total error is U-shaped in complexity — the goal isn't minimizing bias or variance, it's minimizing their sum."
★ Classic killerWhy does L1 regularization produce sparse weights but L2 doesn't?
Two ways to answer — give the geometric one, then the calculus one if pushed.

Geometric: Regularization = minimizing loss subject to a constraint region. L1's region (|w₁|+|w₂| ≤ t) is a diamond with corners on the axes; L2's (w₁²+w₂² ≤ t) is a circle. The optimum sits where the loss contours first touch the region. A diamond's corners stick out, so contours very often touch at a corner — and a corner means some coordinate is exactly zero. A circle has no corners, so the touch point almost never lands exactly on an axis.

Calculus: The L2 penalty gradient is 2w — it shrinks proportionally, so as w → 0 the pushing force → 0 too, and you approach zero asymptotically without arriving. The L1 penalty gradient is λ·sign(w) — constant magnitude regardless of how small w is. That constant push drives weights fully to zero and pins them there. One-liner: "L2 shrinks with a force proportional to w, so it never quite reaches zero. L1 shrinks with a constant force, so it does."
WhyWhy does regularization reduce overfitting at all?
It shrinks the effective hypothesis space. Large weights let a model produce sharp, wiggly decision surfaces that chase individual training points. Penalizing weight magnitude makes those solutions expensive, so the optimizer prefers smoother functions that can't contort to fit noise. Bayesian framing (strong answer if they push): regularization is a prior. L2 = Gaussian prior on the weights; L1 = Laplace prior. Minimizing regularized loss is exactly MAP estimation instead of MLE.
CompareHigh bias vs high variance — how do you diagnose which you have, and what's the fix for each?
Diagnose by comparing training error to validation error:
High bias (underfit)High variance (overfit)
Training errorHighLow
Val errorHigh, close to trainHigh, big gap from train
FixBigger/more complex model, more features, less regularization, train longerMore data, more regularization, fewer features, simpler model, early stopping, dropout/ensembling
The trap: "get more data" only helps variance. More data does nothing for a model too simple to represent the pattern — that's the single most common wrong answer here.
WhyWhy k-fold cross-validation instead of one train/test split? What does k control?
A single split gives a high-variance estimate — your score depends on which points happened to land in test. k-fold averages over k splits, so every point is used for validation exactly once, giving a lower-variance estimate and using all data for training across folds. k controls a bias-variance tradeoff in the estimate itself. Large k (→ LOOCV): each model trains on nearly all data, so the estimate is nearly unbiased, but folds are highly correlated → high variance, and it's expensive. Small k (e.g. 5): cheaper, lower variance, but each model sees less data → pessimistically biased. k=5 or 10 is the standard compromise. Use stratified k-fold for classification (preserves class ratios per fold) and time-series split for temporal data — never shuffle time series, that leaks the future.

Evaluation Metrics

★ Very commonWhy is accuracy a bad metric on imbalanced data? What do you use instead?
With 99% negatives, a model that predicts "negative" for everything scores 99% accuracy and is completely useless — it never finds a single positive. Accuracy is dominated by the majority class. Use precision, recall, F1 (per-class), PR-AUC, or a confusion matrix. Match the metric to the cost: for fraud/disease detection, recall matters most (missing a positive is expensive); for spam filtering, precision matters most (false positives are expensive).
Define + whyPrecision vs recall. Why is F1 the harmonic mean and not the arithmetic mean?
Precision = TP/(TP+FP) — of what I flagged, how much was right. Recall = TP/(TP+FN) — of what was actually there, how much did I catch.

Why harmonic: the harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller value, so it punishes imbalance. Consider precision=1.0, recall=0.0 (you made one correct prediction and missed everything else). Arithmetic mean = 0.5 — misleadingly decent. Harmonic mean = 0 — correctly says the model is useless. "F1 forces you to be good at both. You can't game it by maxing one and ignoring the other."
★ DeepWhat does ROC-AUC actually measure? When does it mislead?
ROC plots TPR vs FPR across all thresholds. AUC has an exact probabilistic meaning: the probability that a randomly chosen positive is ranked above a randomly chosen negative. AUC = 0.5 is random ranking; 1.0 is perfect separation. Because it sweeps thresholds, it measures ranking quality independent of where you set the cutoff. When it misleads: on severe class imbalance. FPR = FP/(FP+TN) has a huge TN in the denominator, so even a large absolute number of false positives barely moves FPR — ROC-AUC stays flatteringly high. Use PR-AUC instead, since precision uses FP/(TP+FP) and has no TN term, making it sensitive to exactly the failure you care about.
AppliedYou lower the classification threshold from 0.5 to 0.3. What happens to precision and recall, and why?
You now predict "positive" more readily → you catch more true positives but also admit more false positives.
Recall goes up (FN decreases). Precision goes down (FP increases). That's the precision-recall tradeoff, and it's why a single threshold-free number (AUC) exists at all. Threshold choice is a business decision, not a modeling one — set it by the relative cost of FP vs FN.

Trees & Ensembles

★ Killer compareBagging vs boosting — what does each reduce, and why?
This is the ensemble question and the answer is one word each.
Bagging (Random Forest)Boosting (AdaBoost, GBM, XGBoost)
ReducesVarianceBias
TrainingParallel — independent modelsSequential — each fixes the last one's errors
Base learnersDeep/complex (low bias, high variance)Weak/shallow stumps (high bias, low variance)
DataBootstrap samplesReweighted (or residuals) each round
Overfitting riskLow — more trees never hurtsHigher — too many rounds overfits
Why bagging cuts variance: averaging n i.i.d. estimators divides variance by n. The trees are individually high-variance but their errors partially cancel. Why boosting cuts bias: each new learner is fit specifically to what the ensemble still gets wrong, so the combined model becomes progressively more expressive.
WhyRandom Forest already bootstraps rows. Why also randomly subsample features at each split?
Because averaging only kills variance if the trees are decorrelated. Variance of the mean of n correlated variables with pairwise correlation ρ is ρσ² + (1-ρ)σ²/n — as n→∞ the second term vanishes but ρσ² remains. So correlation puts a floor on how much averaging can help. If one feature is very predictive, every bootstrapped tree would split on it first and all trees would look alike (high ρ). Forcing each split to consider only a random feature subset breaks that, lowering ρ and pushing the variance floor down. That's the entire difference between Random Forest and plain bagged trees.
CompareEntropy vs Gini impurity — what's the actual difference, and does it matter?
Gini    = 1 - Σ pᵢ²
Entropy = - Σ pᵢ log₂(pᵢ)
Both measure node impurity, both are 0 when pure and maximal at a uniform split. In practice they produce very similar trees — the honest answer is that the choice rarely matters. The real distinction worth stating: Gini is cheaper (no logarithm), which is why it's sklearn's default. Entropy penalizes impurity slightly more aggressively and comes from information theory (information gain = reduction in entropy).
WhyWhy do decision trees overfit so easily, and how do you stop it?
A fully grown tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure — it can memorize the training set exactly, giving zero training error and terrible generalization. They're high-variance because a small change in data changes the split structure. Fixes: pre-pruning (max_depth, min_samples_split, min_samples_leaf), post-pruning (cost-complexity pruning), or ensembling (Random Forest) — which is really just accepting the variance and averaging it away.

SVM · KNN · Naive Bayes

★ DeepExplain the kernel trick. Why is it a "trick"?
SVM's optimization depends on the data only through dot products xᵢ·xⱼ. To handle non-linear boundaries you'd map to a higher-dimensional space φ(x) and work there — but φ(x) can be enormous or even infinite-dimensional (RBF), so computing it explicitly is impossible. The trick: a kernel function computes K(xᵢ,xⱼ) = φ(xᵢ)·φ(xⱼ) directly from the original inputs, without ever constructing φ(x). You get the geometry of the high-dimensional space at the cost of a low-dimensional computation. That's why it's a trick — you never visit the space you're implicitly working in. Common kernels: linear, polynomial, RBF/Gaussian (infinite-dimensional feature space), sigmoid. Valid kernels must satisfy Mercer's condition (produce a PSD kernel matrix).
WhyWhat is SVM actually maximizing, and what does the C parameter control?
It maximizes the margin — the distance from the decision boundary to the nearest points of either class (the support vectors). A wider margin is a more confident, better-generalizing separator; only the support vectors determine the boundary, so the rest of the data can be discarded. C is the soft-margin penalty — how much you punish misclassified/inside-margin points. Small C = wide margin, more violations tolerated → more regularization, higher bias. Large C = narrow margin, few violations tolerated → fits training data hard, higher variance. C is inversely a regularization strength.
★ DeepWhy does KNN break down in high dimensions?
The curse of dimensionality. As dimensions grow, volume grows exponentially, so any fixed number of points becomes vanishingly sparse. Critically, distances concentrate — the ratio between the nearest and farthest neighbor approaches 1: 
lim(d→∞)  (dist_max - dist_min) / dist_min  →  0
When everything is roughly equidistant, "nearest neighbor" carries no information — the entire premise of KNN collapses. This is why you reduce dimensionality (PCA, feature selection) before distance-based methods. Also worth saying: KNN is a lazy learner — no training phase, all cost at inference (O(n·d) per query), and it requires feature scaling since raw magnitude dominates Euclidean distance.
★ DeepWhy is Naive Bayes "naive," and why does it work well anyway?
"Naive" = it assumes all features are conditionally independent given the class. That's almost always false in reality ("New" and "York" in text are obviously dependent). Why it still works: classification only needs the argmax over classes, not calibrated probabilities. The independence assumption badly distorts the probability estimates, but it often distorts them in the same direction for every class — so the ranking, and therefore the predicted label, stays correct. Its probability outputs are poorly calibrated; its decisions are frequently fine. Also: it needs very little data, trains in one pass, and is a strong text-classification baseline. Mention Laplace smoothing to handle unseen feature/class combinations that would otherwise zero out the whole product.
CompareGenerative vs discriminative models — give the distinction and examples.
Discriminative models learn P(y|x) directly — the decision boundary only. Logistic regression, SVM, neural nets, random forests.
Generative models learn the joint P(x,y) (via P(x|y) and P(y)), then apply Bayes' rule to get P(y|x). Naive Bayes, GMMs, LDA, and — relevant to your work — LLMs, which model the distribution of the data itself. Because generative models model the data distribution, they can sample new data; discriminative ones can't. Discriminative usually wins on pure classification accuracy given enough data (they optimize exactly the thing you're scored on); generative can do better with little data since the modeling assumptions act as a prior.

Unsupervised & PCA

★ DeepWhat is PCA actually doing? What do the eigenvectors and eigenvalues mean?
PCA finds an orthogonal basis where the axes are ordered by how much variance the data has along them. You compute the covariance matrix and take its eigenvectors (the principal component directions) and eigenvalues (the variance captured along each). Keep the top k, project onto them, and you've reduced dimensionality while retaining the most variation. The elegant fact worth stating: maximizing retained variance and minimizing squared reconstruction error are the same objective — total variance is fixed, so whatever you don't keep becomes reconstruction error. Two framings, one solution. Musts: scale features first (PCA chases variance, so an unscaled large-magnitude feature dominates spuriously). Components are uncorrelated by construction. Downside: components are linear combinations of all originals, so they're not interpretable — that's the real cost of PCA.
WhyDoes k-means always converge? Does it find the global optimum?
It always converges, but only to a local optimum.
Convergence: each of the two steps (assign points to nearest centroid; move centroid to the mean of its points) can only decrease or hold the within-cluster sum of squares. The objective is bounded below by 0 and there are finitely many assignments, so it must terminate. But the objective is non-convex, so the result depends on initialization — bad seeds give bad clusters. Fixes: run multiple restarts and keep the best (n_init), or use k-means++ seeding, which spreads initial centroids apart probabilistically. Its assumptions (a great follow-up to volunteer): it implicitly assumes clusters are spherical, similarly sized, and equally dense, because it uses Euclidean distance to a centroid. It fails badly on elongated or nested shapes — use DBSCAN or GMM there.
AppliedHow do you choose k in k-means?
Elbow method — plot within-cluster sum of squares vs k and look for the bend where added clusters stop paying off. (WCSS decreases monotonically — at k=n it's zero — so you can't just minimize it.)
Silhouette score — measures how close each point is to its own cluster vs the next nearest, in [-1,1]; pick the k that maximizes it. Usually more reliable than the elbow, which is often ambiguous. Also valid and worth saying: domain knowledge. If the business needs 5 customer segments, k=5 — the metric doesn't override the use case.

Optimization & Theory

CompareBatch vs mini-batch vs stochastic gradient descent — why is mini-batch the default?
Batch uses all n samples per update: accurate gradient, very slow per step, and memory-heavy. SGD uses one sample: fast, noisy, high-variance updates. Mini-batch (32–512) sits between. Mini-batch wins for two reasons: it vectorizes efficiently on GPUs (one sample wastes parallel hardware), and its gradient noise is a feature, not a bug — the noise helps escape sharp local minima and saddle points, and is associated with flatter minima that generalize better. Follow-up: momentum accumulates a velocity term to damp oscillation across ravines; Adam combines momentum with per-parameter adaptive learning rates (RMSProp), which is why it's the common default.
WhyWhy do you need feature scaling? Which algorithms need it and which don't?
Any algorithm using distances or gradient descent is sensitive to feature magnitude. If income (0–100,000) and age (0–100) are raw, income dominates every Euclidean distance and the loss surface becomes a stretched ravine that gradient descent zig-zags down slowly.
Needs scalingDoesn't
KNN, SVM, k-means, PCA, neural nets, ridge/lasso, logistic regression (for convergence speed)Decision trees, Random Forest, gradient-boosted trees
Why trees don't care: they split on thresholds within a single feature ("is age > 30"), which is invariant to monotonic rescaling. They never compare across features on a common scale. Standardization (z-score, mean 0 std 1) vs normalization (min-max to [0,1]) — standardization for roughly-Gaussian data and when outliers exist; min-max when you need a bounded range.
★ DeepMLE vs MAP — what's the relationship, and how does it connect to regularization?
MLE picks parameters maximizing the likelihood of the observed data: argmax P(D|θ). MAP maximizes the posterior: argmax P(D|θ)·P(θ) — likelihood times a prior. The connection interviewers want: MAP = MLE + regularization. Taking logs turns the product into a sum, so the log-prior becomes an additive penalty term. A Gaussian prior on weights yields exactly the L2 penalty; a Laplace prior yields exactly L1. So regularization isn't an ad-hoc hack — it's Bayesian inference with a prior belief that weights should be small. MAP → MLE as data grows, since the likelihood eventually swamps the prior.
AppliedHow do you handle imbalanced datasets?
Four families, and a good answer names the tradeoff of each:
  • Resampling — oversample minority (risks overfitting duplicates) or undersample majority (discards real data)
  • SMOTE — synthesizes new minority points by interpolating between neighbors instead of duplicating; can create unrealistic points in overlapping regions
  • Class weights — penalize minority errors more in the loss (class_weight='balanced'). Often the cleanest fix: no data distortion.
  • Threshold tuning — keep the model, move the decision cutoff using the PR curve
And always: change the metric (PR-AUC, F1, recall — not accuracy) and use stratified splits so folds keep the class ratio. Critical detail: resample only the training fold, never the validation/test set — otherwise you leak synthetic data into evaluation and your scores become fiction.

★ When you blank in the room

This is the part that actually cost you the Comcast round — not missing knowledge so much as not producing it under pressure. Tactics matter as much as content.

Say the structure, then fill it

Even when the precise answer won't come, the shape usually will. "There's a tradeoff here between X and Y" or "this decomposes into two terms" buys you seconds and often unlocks the detail. Interviewers score reasoning, not instant recall.

Anchor to something you've actually built

Your strongest asset is that you've shipped real systems. Route abstract questions through them:
  • Overfitting → "In the transformer I trained, I watched validation loss diverge from training loss — that's the signal."
  • Evaluation → "In the RLM benchmark I ran 3 multi-step stress queries against a single-shot RAG baseline; I'd want a much larger eval set to claim a real number."
  • Embeddings/similarity → your LanceDB vector search work.
  • Bias-variance → the tradeoff between a small model that underfits and a large one memorizing a small corpus.
Concrete beats textbook, and it plays to your actual edge.

"I don't remember the formula, but here's the intuition"

Far stronger than silence, and far stronger than confidently wrong. Most interviewers accept intuition and move on; some will even give you the formula and ask you to reason from it — which turns a dead end into a live question you can win.

Don't let one bad answer cascade

You noted round 2 went worse than round 1. A common cause is that one stumble consumes your attention for the next several questions. Reset explicitly — "let me think about that one properly" — answer it, and let it go. Each question is scored on its own.
